About

01 / Sylhet, Bangladesh

I work on the electronic structure of organic emitters, starting from the geometry. My thesis asks how to hold a donor and an acceptor face to face — close, rigid, and deliberately not joined through a conjugated bridge — so that charge transfer happens through space rather than along a chain.

That geometry is the whole point. Separating the frontier orbitals spatially shrinks the exchange integral, the singlet–triplet splitting collapses, and triplet excitons that would otherwise decay non-radiatively can cross back up to S1. Reaching that regime means optimising ground-state configurations in Gaussian 16 with dispersion-corrected functionals, taking binding and excitation energies from those structures, and reading the character of every transition through hole–electron distribution analysis in Multiwfn.

Before the current triads I worked on a series of donor–acceptor [3.3]paracyclophanes that differ only in the saturated bridge holding the two decks cofacial. That study is where I learned not to trust an excitation energy until I know what the state actually is — and to treat the bridge as an electronic participant rather than a piece of scaffolding.

Separately, I am building a framework that couples SkyNet nucleosynthesis networks to MESA stellar models, so neutrino emission from neutron-capture burning can be followed as a time-resolved output rather than estimated after the fact. Different scale, same habit: understand what the numerical machinery is actually claiming before trusting the number it hands you.

Research statement

02 / What I'm after

The question I keep returning to is how much of a molecule's photophysics is fixed by its geometry rather than by its chemistry.

Emitter design is usually framed as a search for the right functional groups — a stronger acceptor, a bulkier donor, a different heteroatom. But the singlet–triplet gap that decides whether an OLED can recover its triplets is, to a good approximation, twice an exchange integral, and that integral runs over the product of the two frontier orbitals evaluated at the same point in space. It is governed by their overlap. Separate them and the gap collapses, without changing what the molecule is made of.

A small gap is necessary and not sufficient, which is the part I find most interesting. Reverse intersystem crossing also needs spin–orbit coupling, and coupling between a singlet and a triplet of the same charge-transfer character is close to forbidden — the process is generally understood to be mediated by a nearby locally excited triplet. Push donor and acceptor further apart and the gap keeps shrinking while that coupling, and the oscillator strength, shrink with it. The target is not one number but an arrangement of three states, and the usable region is narrow.

That is what draws me to rigid architectures — spiro linkages, paracyclophanes, U-shaped triads. They replace a thermal average over conformations with a geometry you can fix, compute against and, in principle, measure. They also make failure diagnosable: in my paracyclophane work a linker I had been treating as inert scaffolding turned out to carry its own low-lying excited state, and the molecule went dark with the designed transition still intact a few states above it. I no longer trust an excitation energy until I know what the state is made of.

Everything I do now ends at a predicted number. A gap, a rate, an efficiency are only worth what they survive in a device, and my work has so far stopped at the screen. I would rather close that loop than hand a structure over and wait to hear. A group where computed design and fabrication share a building is where I think I would be most useful — and most usefully wrong.

In one line

I use electronic structure theory to work out what molecular geometry does to excited states, with the aim of designing organic emitters that can be built and measured rather than only predicted.

Focus

03 / Three threads
i

Through-space charge transfer

Rigid U-shaped triads that clamp donor and acceptor cofacial, so charge transfer proceeds across the stacking gap instead of along a conjugated bridge — and the orbital overlap that normally enforces a large exchange splitting is designed away.

ii

Gap engineering for RISC

Driving ΔEST below 0.1 eV using dispersion-corrected DFT and TD-DFT so reverse intersystem crossing stays fast enough at room temperature to blunt efficiency roll-off.

iii

Synthesis & device fabrication

Not yet my day-to-day — everything above is computational — but it is where I want to take the work. A predicted ΔEST stays a hypothesis until someone synthesises the emitter, builds the stack and measures what comes out of it, and I would rather close that loop myself than hand the structures over and wait.

iv

Neutrinos from neutron capture

Coupling SkyNet reaction networks to MESA stellar evolution models to follow time-dependent neutrino emission through neutron-capture burning, as a properly resolved output of the network rather than a post-hoc estimate.
