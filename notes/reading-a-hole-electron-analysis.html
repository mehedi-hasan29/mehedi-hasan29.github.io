Reading a hole-electron analysis

Method. 7 min read.

TD-DFT may say state 1 is 92% HOMO → LUMO. That sentence holds almost no physics. It names the two orbitals that dominate. It does not say where they live. It does not say whether charge moved. It does not say if this is your designed charge-transfer state.

For stacked donor-acceptor systems, this matters more than usual. The failure is specific and easy to miss. You get a state that is nominally HOMO → LUMO. It sits at a plausible energy. On inspection, it is a local excitation on one deck. Its singlet-triplet gap is large. It will not do what you want. The orbital labels give no warning.

Hole-electron analysis answers the question directly. It skips orbital pairs. Instead it builds two real-space densities. The hole shows where density was removed. The electron shows where density arrived. Then it measures how the two relate.

Getting the data out of Gaussian

You need two things. One is the wavefunction. The other is the configuration coefficients that define the excitation.

By default, Gaussian cuts off the printed CI coefficients. The cut is harsh enough to distort the densities. Force it to print more:

# TD(NStates=10) <functional>/<basis> IOp(9/40=4)

By default, Gaussian prints only coefficients above 0.1. IOp(9/40=4) lowers this to 0.0001. For a delocalised CT state, the default loses real transition weight. Then convert the checkpoint file:

formchk molecule.chk molecule.fch

Now you have molecule.fch, with the orbitals. You also have molecule.log, with the excitation coefficients. Multiwfn needs both.

The Multiwfn path

Multiwfn molecule.fch

  18   Electron excitation analysis
   1   Analyze and visualize hole-electron distribution,
       transition dipole moment and transition density

  → give the path to molecule.log
  → choose the excited state to analyse

Multiwfn reads the coefficients and builds the hole and electron distributions. It then reports a block of indices. Those indices are the whole point.

What the numbers mean

  • Sr is the overlap of the hole and electron distributions. It comes from the integral of √(ρhole · ρelec). It asks whether the two densities share the same space. A local excitation gives a large Sr. A well-separated charge-transfer state gives a small one. This index links most directly to the exchange integral. So it links most directly to ΔEST.
  • D index is the distance between the hole and electron centroids. A large D means charge really moved across the molecule. In a stacked system, D should match the interplanar separation. That holds if the transfer is truly deck to deck.
  • H index is the average spatial extent of the two distributions. Alone, it only describes. Its real use is as the yardstick for judging D.
  • t index is D minus the extent along the separation axis. Read its sign. t > 0 means hole and electron are truly apart, with a real gap. t < 0 means the centroids are apart but the densities still overlap. Then the state is only partly charge-transfer, however large D looked.
D tells you the centroids moved. t tells you if anything truly separated. A large D with a negative t is possible. That state will not act like a CT state.

Which fragment gave, and which received

For donor-acceptor systems, inter-fragment charge transfer is more decisive. Define the donor and acceptor as atom groups. Multiwfn then reports how much charge moved between them. It gives this as a percentage of the excitation.

This number settles the argument. A true CT state moves most density from donor to acceptor. Sometimes most of the transfer stays inside one fragment. Then the state is a local excitation. The CT label was wishful thinking.

Look at it as well

Multiwfn exports cube files for the hole and electron densities. Render them as isosurfaces. VMD is the usual choice. Put them side by side.

The picture catches what the indices average away. Two states can give similar Sr and D values. One may have a cleanly separated hole and electron. The other may smear its hole across both fragments. Its centroid may just land somewhere convenient. The indices are summary statistics. Summary statistics hide two-peaked shapes. Looking costs ten minutes.

A worked case: the state that was not there

Here is the situation this analysis exists for.

I studied a series of five donor-acceptor [3.3]paracyclophanes. Four members emitted normally. Each kept a HOMO→LUMO charge-transfer transition around 442 to 471 nm. Their oscillator strengths sat near 0.03. The fifth carried a thiocarbonyl bridge. Its emission oscillator strength was two orders of magnitude lower.

The tempting reading is that the CT state was damaged. The bridge upset the coupling, the dipole shrank and brightness fell. That reading is wrong. Yet nothing in the energies or orbital labels shows it.

The character analysis showed the CT transition fully intact. It kept its donor-to-acceptor character. Its total intensity was conserved. It had simply moved from S1 to S3. Two states displaced it, and both ignored the donor and acceptor. Both were bridge-localised n→π* excitations. In each, a sulfur lone pair went into a π* orbital. Emission comes from the lowest excited state. So the molecule went dark. Its designed transition still worked, two states higher.

You cannot see that in an energy. You see it once you ask where the hole is.

Picture the hole on bridge sulfur, the electron on its π*. Next to a donor hole and an acceptor electron, it is unmistakable. The two states look nothing alike. But their orbital labels are equally unhelpful. And their energies are close enough to swap when the bridge changes.

Atom-resolved population analysis, when densities are not enough

Hole-electron distributions are spatial. Sometimes the question is per atom. Then a Hirshfeld partition of the key orbitals is the sharper tool.

In the same series, it showed something the isosurfaces had smoothed over. The two bridge sulfurs are equivalent by nominal symmetry. Yet they contributed unequally to individual bridge-localised orbitals. The geometry shows no measurable asymmetry, though. I report this descriptively. The present data do not settle its origin.

A result like that deserves a pause. We expect symmetric molecules to give symmetric orbitals. When they do not, it is either physics or a broken calculation. You have to find out which.

Do it for the triplet too

Most people run this on S1 and stop. That throws away half of what the method offers.

Run it on the low-lying triplets too, and compare. Say T1 is a local excitation on one fragment. Say S1 is charge-transfer. Then they are not simple spin partners. They differ in spatial character. Your computed gap is not the barrier the thermal process sees.

Here the analysis turns from descriptive to predictive. Take a 1CT and a 3CT state of the same spatial character. Reverse intersystem crossing between them is roughly spin-orbit forbidden. A nearby locally excited triplet is thought to mediate it. It mixes in through vibronic coupling. So a 3LE state near your CT states is not a contaminant. It may be why the emitter works at all.

A short checklist

  1. Run TD-DFT with IOp(9/40=4). Use a range-separated functional suited to CT states.
  2. Run formchk on the checkpoint.
  3. In Multiwfn, choose 18 then 1, for S1 and the low triplets.
  4. Record Sr, D, H and t for each state. Use one table and the same units.
  5. Run the inter-fragment analysis with donor and acceptor defined explicitly.
  6. Export the cubes and really look at the isosurfaces.
  7. Check enough states, not just S1. Your designed transition may no longer be lowest.
  8. Only then give the state a label.

The discipline matters more than any single index. It stops you carrying a wrong assumption through a whole study. Otherwise you may learn the truth only while writing up.
