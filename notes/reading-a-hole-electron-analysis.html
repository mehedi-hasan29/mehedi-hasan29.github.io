Reading a hole–electron analysis

Method · 7 min read

A TD-DFT output will happily tell you that state 1 is 92% HOMO → LUMO. That sentence contains almost no physics. It tells you which two orbitals dominate the transition, but not where those orbitals live, not whether charge actually moved, and not whether the state is the charge-transfer state you designed the molecule to have.

For a stacked donor–acceptor system this matters more than usual, because the failure mode is specific and easy to miss: you get a state that is nominally HOMO → LUMO, sits at a plausible energy, and turns out on inspection to be a local excitation confined to one deck. It will have a large singlet–triplet gap and it will not do what you want. The orbital labels give you no warning.

Hole–electron analysis is the tool that answers the question directly. Instead of describing the transition through orbital pairs, it constructs two real-space densities — where the excitation removed density (the hole) and where it deposited density (the electron) — and then measures the relationship between them.

Getting the data out of Gaussian

Two things are needed: the wavefunction, and the configuration coefficients that define the excitation.

Gaussian truncates the printed CI coefficients by default, and the truncation is aggressive enough to distort the reconstructed densities. Force it to print more:

# TD(NStates=10) <functional>/<basis> IOp(9/40=4)

IOp(9/40=4) prints coefficients down to 10−4. The default of 2 keeps only those above 10−2, which for a delocalised CT state can discard a meaningful fraction of the transition. Then convert the checkpoint file:

formchk molecule.chk molecule.fch

You now have molecule.fch (the orbitals) and molecule.log (the excitation coefficients). Multiwfn needs both.

The Multiwfn path

Multiwfn molecule.fch

  18   Electron excitation analysis
   1   Analyze and visualize hole-electron distribution,
       transition dipole moment and transition density

  → give the path to molecule.log
  → choose the excited state to analyse

Multiwfn reads the coefficients, builds the hole and electron distributions, and reports a block of indices. Those indices are the point of the exercise.

What the numbers mean

  • Sr — the overlap between the hole and electron distributions, formed from the integral of √(ρhole · ρelec). It answers "do these two densities occupy the same space?" A local excitation gives a large Sr; a well-separated charge-transfer state gives a small one. This is the index most directly connected to the exchange integral, and therefore to ΔEST.
  • D index — the distance between the centroid of the hole and the centroid of the electron. Large D means charge physically moved across the molecule. For a stacked system, D should be comparable to the interplanar separation if the transfer really is deck-to-deck.
  • H index — the average spatial extent of the two distributions. On its own it is descriptive; its value is as the reference against which D is judged.
  • t index — defined as D minus the extent of the distributions along the separation axis. The sign is what you read. t > 0 means the hole and electron are genuinely separated, with a real gap between them. t < 0 means their centroids are apart but the distributions still overlap — the state is only partially charge-transfer, however large D looked.
D tells you the centroids moved. t tells you whether anything actually separated. It is entirely possible to have a large D and a negative t, and that state will not behave like a CT state.

Which fragment gave, and which received

For a donor–acceptor system, the more decisive analysis is inter-fragment charge transfer. Define the donor and acceptor as atom groups and Multiwfn will report how much charge moved between them, as a percentage of the excitation.

This is the number that settles the argument. A state described as CT should show most of its density transferring from the donor fragment to the acceptor fragment. If the analysis instead shows the bulk of the transfer happening within a single fragment, the state is a local excitation and the CT label was wishful.

Look at it as well

Multiwfn will export cube files for the hole and electron densities. Render them as isosurfaces — VMD is the usual choice — and put them side by side.

The picture catches things the indices average away. Two states can return similar Sr and D while one has a cleanly separated hole and electron and the other has a hole smeared across both fragments with a centroid that happens to land in a convenient place. The indices are summary statistics, and summary statistics hide bimodality. It costs ten minutes to look.

A worked case: the state that wasn't there

Here is the situation this analysis exists for.

In a series of five donor–acceptor [3.3]paracyclophanes, four members emitted normally — a conserved HOMO→LUMO charge-transfer transition around 442–471 nm with oscillator strength near 0.03. The fifth, carrying a thiocarbonyl bridge, showed an emission oscillator strength two orders of magnitude lower.

The tempting reading is that the CT state was damaged: the bridge perturbed the donor–acceptor coupling, the transition dipole shrank, brightness fell. That reading is wrong, and nothing in the excitation energies or the orbital labels distinguishes it from the right one.

What the character analysis showed is that the CT transition was completely intact — same composition, same oscillator strength as its siblings. It had simply been displaced from S1 to S3 by a state that had nothing to do with the donor or the acceptor: a bridge-localised n→π* excitation, a sulfur lone pair promoted into a π* orbital, sitting on the linker. Emission comes from the lowest excited state, so the molecule went dark with its designed transition in perfect working order a couple of states up.

You cannot see that in an energy. You see it the moment you ask where the hole is.

A hole on the bridge sulfur and an electron on the bridge π* is unmistakable next to a hole on the donor deck and an electron on the acceptor deck. The two states do not resemble each other at all — but their orbital labels are similarly unhelpful, and their excitation energies are close enough to swap order when you change the bridge.

Atom-resolved population analysis, when densities aren't enough

Hole–electron distributions are spatial. Sometimes the question is per-atom, and then a Hirshfeld partition of the relevant orbitals is the sharper tool.

In the same series it revealed something the isosurfaces alone would have smoothed over: the two bridge sulfurs, which are equivalent by the molecule's nominal symmetry, contributed unequally to the bridge-localised orbitals. Genuine symmetry breaking — present already at the vertical Franck–Condon geometry, and amplified after optimising the S1 structure.

That is the kind of result worth pausing on, because the instinct is to assume a converged calculation on a symmetric molecule produces symmetric orbitals. When it does not, the asymmetry is either physics or a broken calculation, and you have to establish which. Reproducing the effect under additional range-separated functionals is what turns it from a suspicion into a finding.

Do it for the triplet too

Most people run this analysis on S1 and stop, which throws away half of what the method can tell you.

Run it on the low-lying triplets as well, and compare the character. If T1 comes back as a local excitation on one fragment while S1 is charge-transfer, the two states are not simply spin partners — they differ in spatial character, and the energy separation you computed is not the barrier a thermally activated process actually sees.

That comparison is also where the analysis stops being descriptive and starts being predictive. Reverse intersystem crossing between a 1CT and a 3CT state of identical spatial character is spin–orbit forbidden to a good approximation; the process is generally understood to be mediated by a nearby locally-excited triplet mixing in through vibronic coupling. So finding a 3LE state close in energy to your CT manifold is not a contaminant in the results. It may be the reason the emitter works at all.

A short checklist

  1. TD-DFT with IOp(9/40=4), and a functional appropriate for CT states rather than whichever hybrid was used for the geometry.
  2. formchk the checkpoint.
  3. Multiwfn → 18 → 1, for S1 and for the low triplets.
  4. Record Sr, D, H and t for each state, in a table, in the same units.
  5. Run the inter-fragment analysis with donor and acceptor defined explicitly.
  6. Export the cubes and actually look at the isosurfaces.
  7. Check enough states, not just S1. The transition you designed may not be the lowest one any more.
  8. Only then assign the state a label.

The discipline this enforces is worth more than any individual index. It stops you from carrying an assumption about what a state is through an entire study, and finding out at the writing-up stage that the molecule was doing something else.
