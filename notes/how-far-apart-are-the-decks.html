How far apart are the decks?

Paracyclophane geometry. 7 min read.

I was describing my [3.3]paracyclophane work. I wrote that longer bridges push the two decks further apart. It felt natural. A longer tether should hold things further away. And every textbook stresses how strained [2.2]paracyclophane is.

So I opened my own optimised geometry to get a number. The centroid-centroid separation was 3.118 Å. My sentence was wrong.

It took longer to see the deeper problem. My sentence asked: are [3.3] decks further apart than [2.2] decks? That question has no answer yet. Three or four other things must be fixed first. A better number alone would not fix the reasoning.

[2.2]paracyclophane does not have a deck separation

The two benzene rings in [2.2]paracyclophane are not flat. The ethylene bridges are too short to span them comfortably. So the rings bow. The bridgehead carbons pull towards each other. The four unbridged carbons splay away. Each deck is boat-shaped. One boat does not sit at a single distance from another.

That is why the literature gives more than one number. A 2020 review in Angewandte Chemie gives a mean of 3.09 Å. It also says the bridges hold the bridgehead carbons at 2.83 Å. One computational study asks if the transannular interaction is repulsive or attractive. It quotes 2.798 Å between opposing bridgehead carbons. It quotes 3.102 Å from the non-bridging carbons. That is one molecule and one structure. Yet the two distances differ by three tenths of an Ångström. A third study used an optimised geometry, not an experimental one. It reports an interplanar distance of 3.153 Å.

None of these numbers disagree. They answer different questions. Quoting the [2.2] separation means quietly choosing one. That choice moves the number more than my claimed effect.

The decks do not have one separation. They have a set of distances. Which one you quote is a choice, not a measurement.

What my own structure gives

My series is a donor-acceptor [3.3]paracyclophane. A benzene deck faces a benzothiadiazole deck. Two −CH2−X−CH2− bridges hold them cofacial. X is CH2, C=O, C=NH, CF2 or C=S. I optimised all five at MP2/6-31G(d) in the gas phase. Their centroid separation spans only 3.12 to 3.16 Å. For the thiocarbonyl member:

centroid-centroid separation   3.118 Å
closest interdeck contact      2.980 Å
deck planes, angle between     2.57°

It would be easy to call these decks flat, unlike [2.2]. That is not true. Each deck bows towards the other. This boat-boat fold is typical of [3.3] hetero-cyclophanes. It is milder than in [2.2], but it is measurable. The decks are parallel to within 2.57°. So a centroid separation is a fair summary. But it is not the only distance. The closest contact is 0.14 Å shorter. My structure has the same problem as [2.2], only milder. The number still needs its definition.

Against other [3.3] structures

My value is also short for its own family. One dithia[3.3]paracyclophane was solved by X-ray crystallography. It is 5,8-dibromo-14,17-difluoro-2,11-dithia[3.3]paracyclophane. Its centroid-centroid distance is 3.3066(18) Å. Its rings are 1.53(15)° from parallel.

Beside that, 3.118 Å looks tight. But three things differ at once. Mine is a gas-phase optimisation, and theirs is a crystal structure. Mine has a benzothiadiazole on one deck, not a second benzene. Mine comes from MP2, not from diffraction. Any one of these could explain the gap. So the honest claim is narrow. My structure is tighter than that one structure. It does not show that [3.3] paracyclophanes are tighter than thought.

What to state before comparing two such numbers

  • Which distance. Centroid-centroid, plane to plane, closest contact, or bridgehead to bridgehead. On a bowed deck, these are four different quantities. They do not track each other.
  • Gas phase or crystal. A lone-molecule optimisation answers a different question. A room-temperature X-ray structure answers another. Packing can squeeze or stretch a stack. Thermal motion blurs what diffraction sees.
  • What made the geometry. Dispersion sets the interdeck separation. So a method without dispersion gets exactly this wrong. MP2, a dispersion-corrected functional and a bare global hybrid will disagree. Most of that disagreement lands on this one coordinate.
  • What is on the decks. Two benzenes differ from a benzene and a benzothiadiazole. Substituted versions differ again. Electrostatics between the decks shifts the distance, whatever the bridge.
On the van der Waals reference Both [2.2] and [3.3] decks are said to sit in close contact. The usual reference is van der Waals contact, about 3.4 Å. That is two carbon van der Waals radii. It is also close to graphite's interlayer spacing. It is a useful rough marker and nothing more. It is defined for two isolated carbon atoms. It is not defined for two π systems in a covalent clamp. It says nothing about what the orbitals do at that distance.

Why this is not pedantry

In my own series, the deck separation barely moves. It changes by 0.04 Å across five bridges. Yet the thiocarbonyl member's emission oscillator strength falls over two-hundred-fold. So deck distance does not control the photophysics there. My thesis reaches the same answer from another molecule. Along its soft donor-acceptor torsion, stacking distance tracks neither quantity. Not the exchange integral, and not the transition dipole.

So distance does not control everything. The reason for care is different. Geometric claims get reused. They become comparisons, design rules and reasons for the next calculation. A number without its definition, phase and method cannot carry that weight. A bridge-length claim without those three is not a weak claim. It is not yet a claim.

I did not need a better number. I needed a better question. Not which scaffold holds its decks further apart. Instead, what does my structure do, measured one stated way?
