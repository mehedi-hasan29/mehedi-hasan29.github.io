Charge transfer without a bridge

Through-space charge transfer. 8 min read.

Every OLED starts with a bookkeeping problem. Electrical injection makes singlet and triplet excitons. Spin statistics fix the ratio at one singlet to three triplets. A normal fluorescent emitter only radiates from the singlet. So three quarters of its excitons are dark from the start.

Thermally activated delayed fluorescence tries to win back that 75%. Suppose the lowest triplet T1 sits close to the lowest singlet S1. Then room-temperature heat can push the triplet back up. This step is called reverse intersystem crossing. The exciton then emits from S1 as delayed fluorescence. The whole design problem comes down to one quantity.

The gap is an overlap integral in disguise

The singlet-triplet splitting is roughly twice an exchange integral. That integral runs over the orbitals in the excitation:

ΔE_ST = E(S₁) − E(T₁) ≈ 2K

K = ∫∫ φ_H(r₁) φ_L(r₁) · (1/r₁₂) · φ_H(r₂) φ_L(r₂) dr₁ dr₂

It holds only when S1 and T1 share one orbital pair.

Read the integrand carefully. It holds the product φ_H · φ_L at one point in space, twice. If HOMO and LUMO share a region, that product is large. Then K is large too. If they live in different regions, the product is near zero. Then K collapses with it.

You do not really tune the gap as an energy. You engineer a spatial overlap, and the energy follows.

So the brief is simple to state. Separate the frontier orbitals in space. But keep enough coupling for the transition to stay bright. These two goals pull against each other. Almost every TADF design is a different compromise between them.

The usual answer, and what it costs

The standard approach bonds a donor to an acceptor. Then it twists the linking bond towards 90°. At a large twist, conjugation across the bond fades. The HOMO retreats onto the donor. The LUMO retreats onto the acceptor. ΔEST falls. This works, and most well-known TADF emitters use it.

It also brings structural problems, not electronic ones:

  • The twist is soft. The torsional potential around a single bond is shallow. At room temperature, the molecule samples a spread of angles. ΔEST depends steeply on that angle. So the ensemble holds a spread of gaps. Emission broadens. Molecules stuck at a bad angle undergo RISC poorly.
  • Floppy modes open dark channels. Low-frequency torsions couple the excited state to the ground state. That gives the exciton a way out that is not light.
  • Some conjugation always leaks through. Even at 90°, the through-bond path is weakened, not removed. Hyperconjugation keeps some coupling alive.

Stack them instead

The alternative skips the shared π-system entirely. Donor and acceptor sit face to face on separate π-planes. Coupling then crosses the gap between the planes. It goes through space, not through bonds. The orbitals are separated by design, not by a torsional compromise. The separation becomes a distance you can design. It is no longer an angle the molecule bargains over with heat.

Paracyclophanes are the classic example. In [2.2]paracyclophane, two ethylene bridges clamp two benzene decks face to face. This forces the decks about 2.8 to 3.1 Å apart. The value depends on which atoms are measured. That is inside normal van der Waals contact. Graphite layers sit around 3.4 Å. The strain shows in the rings, which bow out of plane. Lengthen the bridges to three atoms, as in [3.3]paracyclophane. The rings relax towards flatness. The decks still stay inside van der Waals contact. That scaffold is less strained and easier to make. It still keeps the two π systems in close contact.

Now put a donor on one deck and an acceptor opposite. The pair physically cannot rotate apart. That rigidity is why the synthesis is worth the effort. It removes the spread of shapes that blurs twisted emitters. And any coupling left is truly a stacking interaction. It is not a leftover through-bond path in disguise.

Note what the bridges are made of. In a paracyclophane they are saturated sp3 carbons. They hold the decks in place. They do not carry π density between them. So the charge transfer really does go through space. The bridge is only structural.

Except when the bridge stops being structural

That last sentence is where I got caught. The failure hides in the design. So it is worth spelling out.

I worked on a series of five donor-acceptor [3.3]paracyclophanes. Each had a benzene donor and a benzothiadiazole acceptor. The bridges had the form −CH2−X−CH2−. X was CH2, C=O, C=NH, CF2 or C=S. Four of the five behaved as the picture above predicts. Each kept the same HOMO→LUMO emission, at 442 to 471 nm. Oscillator strengths sat between 0.029 and 0.035. The variation did not even track bridge electronegativity in order. That was already a hint. The bridge was not just tuning the deck-to-deck interaction.

The thiocarbonyl member broke the pattern. Its emission oscillator strength fell 212 to 251-fold against the rest.

The emissive transition had not been damaged. It had been demoted.

The charge-transfer state was still there. It kept its donor-to-acceptor character. Its total intensity was conserved. It was simply no longer S1. Two dark states sat on the bridge instead. Each promoted a sulfur lone pair into a π* orbital. These n→π* transitions have nothing to do with the donor or acceptor. They dropped below the bright state and pushed it up to S3. Emission comes from the lowest excited state. So the molecule went dark. Yet every designed property of the bright state stayed intact.

Two details made that conclusion hold. First, the radiative rate alone explains the lost brightness. It fell by a factor of 260 to 349. No non-radiative channel was needed to balance the books. Second, two more range-separated functionals reproduced the reordering. So it was not an artifact of one functional.

One more consequence is worth flagging, not claiming. The intruding states have n→π* character. So El-Sayed's rules make an extra intersystem-crossing path plausible. A spin flip needs a change in orbital angular momentum. An n→π*/π→π* pairing supplies exactly that. This is a reasonable expectation, not a measured result.

The lesson reaches beyond thioketones. Any bridge with a heteroatom lone pair can cause this. So can any bridge with a low-lying π*. It can bring its own excited state into your region. You can engineer the deck separation beautifully. You can still lose the emitter to a state you never watched.

There is a window, and both edges are bad

Interplanar distance is the master variable. Its effect is not monotonic in anything useful.

Too far apart, and the wavefunctions stop overlapping. ΔEST becomes pleasingly small. But the transition dipole between the CT states vanishes too. The emitter is dim. The spin-orbit coupling that drives RISC becomes very weak. A small gap with no coupling is not a TADF emitter. It is a molecule with a long-lived dark state.

Too close, and the deck orbitals mix strongly. The frontier orbitals spread across both rings. The engineered separation disappears. The exchange integral recovers. The gap opens up again.

The useful region sits in between, and it is narrow. Here dispersion correction stops being a formality. It decides your answer. A functional without it misses the stacking distance. A geometry off by tenths of an ångström spoils the rest. Every excited-state property inherits the error.

A note on functionals Charge-transfer energies are a known weak point of TD-DFT with global hybrids. These functionals tend to underestimate them badly. The electron and hole sit far apart. The functional misses the correct −1/R long-range behaviour. Range-separated hybrids are the standard fix. The Tamm-Dancoff approximation is a separate issue. It is often used to avoid triplet instabilities. TDA gives higher triplet energies than full TD-DFT. Full TD-DFT can put triplets too low near an instability. So the choice can move ΔEST a lot. In my emitter, it moved a local triplet strongly. It barely changed the charge-transfer gap. Both choices move the number you care about. So both belong in the methods, not the supplement.

What actually limits the device

A small gap is needed but far from enough. The rate that matters is k_RISC. It depends on spin-orbit coupling as much as on the gap.

The accepted picture here is a little counterintuitive. Consider a singlet CT state and a triplet CT state. If they share spatial character, their spin-orbit coupling is near zero. They differ only in spin. The spin-flip operator needs a change in orbital angular momentum. A nearby locally excited triplet rescues the process. Vibronic coupling mixes it into the CT states. It supplies the orbital character the spin flip needs.

So TADF design is a three-state problem, not a two-state one. You place 1CT, 3CT and 3LE relative to each other. Chasing ΔEST alone can backfire. You may get a tiny gap but useless RISC. Slow RISC lets triplets pile up under drive current. That causes triplet-triplet annihilation. That causes the efficiency roll-off TADF was meant to avoid.

This argues for building the geometry on purpose, not discovering it. Let the scaffold fix the separation. Then change the electronic structure around it. Then you know the change you made is what mattered.

It also argues against labelling a state from orbital composition alone. I only caught this by checking where hole and electron sat. I did that state by state. That is the subject of the companion note on hole-electron analysis.
