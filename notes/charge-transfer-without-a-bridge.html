Charge transfer without a bridge

Through-space charge transfer · 8 min read

Every organic light-emitting diode starts with a bookkeeping problem. Electrical injection produces singlet and triplet excitons in a ratio fixed by spin statistics: one singlet for every three triplets. A conventional fluorescent emitter can only radiate from the singlet, so three quarters of the excitons it makes are dark before the molecule has done anything wrong.

Thermally activated delayed fluorescence is the attempt to get that 75% back. If the lowest triplet T1 sits close enough in energy to the lowest singlet S1, ambient thermal energy can drive the triplet back up to the singlet — reverse intersystem crossing — where it radiates as delayed fluorescence. The whole design problem reduces to one quantity.

The gap is an overlap integral in disguise

The singlet–triplet splitting is, to a good first approximation, twice the exchange integral between the orbitals involved in the excitation:

ΔE_ST = E(S₁) − E(T₁) ≈ 2K

K = ∫∫ φ_H(r₁) φ_L(r₁) · (1/r₁₂) · φ_H(r₂) φ_L(r₂) dr₁ dr₂

Read that integrand carefully. It contains the product φ_H · φ_L evaluated at the same point in space, twice. If the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied orbitals occupy the same region, the product is large everywhere and so is K. If they occupy different regions, the product is close to zero almost everywhere and K collapses along with it.

The gap is not really an energy you tune. It is a spatial overlap you engineer, and the energy follows.

So the design brief is: separate the frontier orbitals in space, while keeping enough electronic communication that the transition still carries oscillator strength. Those two goals pull in opposite directions, and essentially every TADF architecture is a different compromise between them.

The usual answer, and what it costs

The standard approach is to bond a donor to an acceptor and twist the connecting bond towards 90°. At a large dihedral, conjugation across the bond is suppressed, the HOMO retreats onto the donor and the LUMO onto the acceptor, and ΔEST falls. This works, and most of the well-known TADF emitters are built this way.

It also brings problems that are structural rather than electronic:

  • The twist is soft. The torsional potential around a single bond is shallow. At room temperature the molecule samples a distribution of dihedral angles, and since ΔEST depends steeply on that angle, the ensemble contains a distribution of gaps. Emission broadens, and the population that happens to sit at an unfavourable angle does not undergo RISC efficiently.
  • Floppy modes open non-radiative channels. Low-frequency torsions couple the excited state to the ground state and give the exciton somewhere to go that is not light.
  • Some conjugation always leaks through. Even at 90° the through-bond pathway is suppressed, not eliminated, and hyperconjugation keeps a residual coupling alive.

Stack them instead

The alternative is to stop connecting donor and acceptor through the π-system at all, and instead hold them face to face on separate π-planes. Coupling then happens across the gap between the planes — through space rather than through bonds. The orbitals are separated by construction, not by a torsional compromise, and the separation is a distance you can design rather than an angle the molecule negotiates with thermal energy.

Paracyclophanes are the archetype. In [2.2]paracyclophane two benzene decks are clamped face to face by two ethylene bridges, which forces the deck separation to roughly 3.0–3.1 Å. That is inside ordinary van der Waals contact — graphite layers sit around 3.4 Å — and the strain shows in the rings themselves, which bow out of planarity. Lengthen the bridges to three atoms, as in [3.3]paracyclophane, and the rings relax back towards planarity while the decks stay inside van der Waals contact — a less strained and more synthetically forgiving scaffold that still holds the two π systems in close communication.

Either way, mount a donor on one deck and an acceptor on the other and you have a pair that physically cannot rotate away from each other. That rigidity is the reason the architecture is worth the synthetic effort: it removes the conformational distribution that blurs twisted emitters, and it means whatever coupling survives is genuinely a stacking interaction rather than a residual through-bond pathway wearing a disguise.

And note what the bridges are made of. In a paracyclophane they are saturated — sp3 carbons that hold the decks in position without conducting π density between them. The charge transfer really does go through space. The bridge is structural.

Except when the bridge stops being structural

That last sentence is where I got caught, and it is worth spelling out because the failure is not obvious from the design.

In a series of five donor–acceptor [3.3]paracyclophanes I worked on — benzene donor, benzothiadiazole acceptor, bridges of the form −CH2−X−CH2− with X = CH2, C=O, C=NH, CF2 or C=S — four of the five behaved exactly as the picture above predicts. Same structurally conserved HOMO→LUMO emission, 442–471 nm, oscillator strength between 0.029 and 0.035. The variation across those four did not even track bridge electronegativity monotonically, which is already a hint that the bridge is not simply modulating the deck-to-deck interaction.

The thiocarbonyl member broke. Its emission oscillator strength collapsed by a factor of 212–251 relative to the rest of the series.

The emissive transition had not been damaged. It had been demoted.

The charge-transfer state was still there, still structurally conserved, still with the oscillator strength it should have had. It was simply no longer S1. A dark state localised on the bridge — a sulfur lone pair promoted into a π* orbital, an n→π* transition that has nothing to do with the donor or the acceptor — had dropped below it, pushing the emissive state up to S3. Emission happens from the lowest excited state, so the molecule went dark while every property of the state you designed remained intact.

Two details made that conclusion hold up. First, the drop in brightness was fully accounted for by suppression of the radiative rate constant alone, by a factor of 260–349, with no need to invoke a non-radiative decay channel to balance the books. Second, the reordering reproduced under two further range-separated functionals, so it was not an artifact of one functional's treatment of charge transfer.

There is a further consequence worth flagging rather than claiming. Because the intruding states are n→π* in character, El–Sayed's rules make an additional intersystem-crossing pathway plausible — the orbital angular momentum change that a spin flip needs is exactly what an n→π*/π→π* pairing supplies. That is a reasonable expectation, not a measured result.

The design lesson generalises past thioketones: any bridge carrying a heteroatom lone pair or a low-lying π* is a candidate to introduce its own excited state into the region you are trying to control. You can engineer the deck-to-deck separation beautifully and still lose the emitter to a state that lives somewhere you were not looking.

There is a window, and both edges are bad

Interplanar distance is the master variable, and the relationship is not monotonic in anything useful.

Too far apart and the wavefunctions stop overlapping altogether. ΔEST becomes gratifyingly small, but the transition dipole between the CT states also vanishes — the emitter is dim, and the spin–orbit coupling that has to mediate RISC becomes vanishingly weak. A small gap with no coupling across it is not a TADF emitter; it is a molecule with a long-lived dark state.

Too close and the deck orbitals mix strongly enough that the frontier orbitals delocalise across both rings. The separation you engineered disappears, the exchange integral recovers, and the gap opens back up.

The useful region sits in between, and in practice it is narrow. This is also where dispersion correction stops being a formality in the calculation and becomes the thing that determines your answer: a functional without it will not reproduce the equilibrium stacking distance, and if the geometry is wrong by a few tenths of an ångström then every excited-state property computed on top of it is wrong too.

A note on functionals Charge-transfer excitation energies are a known weak point of conventional TD-DFT with global hybrids, which tend to underestimate them badly because the electron and hole are far apart and the functional does not recover the correct −1/R asymptotic behaviour. Range-separated hybrids are the standard remedy. Separately, the Tamm–Dancoff approximation is often applied to avoid triplet instabilities, but it systematically raises triplet energies, which inflates the computed ΔEST. Both choices move the number you care about, so both belong in the methods section rather than the supplementary information.

What actually limits the device

A small gap is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. The rate that matters is k_RISC, and it depends on spin–orbit coupling between the states as much as on the energy separating them.

Here the field's accepted picture is slightly counterintuitive. Spin–orbit coupling between a singlet CT state and a triplet CT state of the same spatial character is close to zero — the two states differ only in spin, and the operator that flips spin needs a change in orbital angular momentum to work with. What rescues the process is a locally excited triplet lying nearby in energy: vibronic coupling mixes it into the CT manifold and provides the orbital character that the spin flip requires.

The practical consequence is that designing a TADF emitter is a three-state problem, not a two-state one. You are positioning 1CT, 3CT and 3LE relative to each other. Optimising ΔEST alone can produce a molecule with a beautifully small gap and a RISC rate too slow to matter — and slow RISC means triplets accumulate under drive current, which is exactly the condition that produces triplet–triplet annihilation and the efficiency roll-off the whole exercise was meant to avoid.

Which is the argument for building the geometry deliberately rather than discovering it. If the scaffold fixes the separation, you can move the electronic structure around it and know that the thing you changed is the thing that mattered.

Which is also the argument for never labelling a state from its orbital composition alone. Catching this required looking at where the hole and electron actually sat, state by state — the subject of the companion note on hole–electron analysis.
